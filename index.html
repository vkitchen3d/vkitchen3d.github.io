Build notes

  1. Dimensional fidelity. Banner runs, rigging drops, wall heights, the header band, monitor and graphic placement — modeled 1:1 off the dimensioned set rather than approximated by eye. The drawing was the requirement, so the model had to survive being measured against it. Dimensions came off the blueprint and went into Maya as exact millimeter values at 1:1 — no scaling, no eyeballing, no "close enough."
  2. Brand compliance. Logo lockups, the navy and yellow, headline typography and the product grid reproduced to the standard. On client work the brand spec is as binding as the dimensions, and a render that drifts on either is useless for approval.
  3. A set, not a hero shot. Elevations are flat and unlit — nobody can tell from them what the stand will actually feel like. Taking the mockup to full 3D from every angle lets the client see the impact of the booth and sign off on the layout before anything gets built. Six cameras covering both approaches, the graphic wall and the interior meeting room, delivered in two weeks and approved after one round.
  4. Separating the feedback from the action item. The note back on the first pass was to make it "a bit more realistic," which could have meant almost anything. The follow-up pinned it down: the client wanted people and a vehicle added to the scene — assets to source — while the actionable item on my side was the atmosphere. The pass they'd seen was unlit, so I did a lighting pass: directional key plus enough fill to stand in for ambient. Vague feedback usually contains more than one request, and the first job is working out which of them is yours.

Circus Magus: The Apprentice — turnaround against concept

Front and back of the sculpt, shown either side of the concept art it was built from. One reference image in a single three-quarter pose — the whole back of the costume had to be reasoned out from it. Presented as a high-poly sculpt; this was a skills-development piece, not a game asset.

Build notes

  1. Silhouette first. The concept lives or dies on outline — twin hair volumes, the sash mass, the skirt profile. Those were locked in blockout and checked against the reference before any detail went on.
  2. Reasoning past the reference. The source is a single three-quarter pose, so the whole back had to be resolved by inference. The sash reads as a front drape in the concept, so I tied it off as a gathered bow with asymmetric tails — that keeps the mass where the silhouette needs it instead of flattening out behind. The bodice closes along the spine rather than gaining fastenings the drawing never showed, and the legwear carries its pattern around unbroken. The rule throughout was to extend what the reference implies and invent nothing it doesn't.
  3. Holding the asymmetry. Mismatched legwear, off-center closures and the star motif are easy to accidentally normalize. Keeping deliberate irregularity intact is a discipline problem, not a modeling one.

On the source

Concept by Marie Magny, used with permission. It's professional character design, which raises the bar on the match rather than lowering it — the asymmetries, the motif placement and the palette splits are all deliberate choices, so none of them could be quietly simplified to make the build easier.

Presented as a sculpt. Retopology and a game mesh weren't in scope for this piece.

Growth Mentor — mascot from a written spec

Three written requirements, no reference art: read as mystical, derive from an animal, and incorporate the client's wheat-stalk mark. Designed and built against those. Shown at model stage with base materials.

Left: my concept pass, developed from the written brief. Right: the model built from it.

Build notes

  1. The animal was an argument, not a preference. The brief asked for something mystical, an animal, the client's mark integrated — and it emphasized change and growth. That last word picked the species: a frog is the textbook metamorphosis, an animal whose whole identity is becoming something else. Mystical came through the hat, grimoire and staff.
  2. The logo became the design. The client's wheat-stalk mark is the staff finial and the emblem on the book cover — carried as a structural element rather than stamped onto a surface, so it survives being seen from any angle.
  3. Concept to final in one week. Design pass, model and presentation turnaround inside the competition deadline, working from the written brief alone.

Why this one is here

The other sheets show fidelity to supplied artwork. This one shows the case where the brief is a sentence and the picture doesn't exist yet — and the result still has to satisfy every stated requirement.

Shown at model stage rather than final render — labeled so, deliberately.

Build notes
